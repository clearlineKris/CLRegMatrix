# Ohio Reg & Pol — Policy Rationale

## Overview

Ohio's cannabis regulatory framework was created by voter approval of Issue 2 in November 2023 and is being actively contested by the Republican supermajority legislature that opposed it. The policy tensions here are not primarily about ambiguous statutory language — they are about **active inter-branch conflict between ballot-box democracy and legislative preference**. Operators must navigate three separate, sometimes contradictory layers: the Issue 2 statute, the legislative amendments, and the DCC's implementing interpretations.

---

## Regulatory Timeline

### November 2023: Issue 2 Passes
- Adult-use cannabis legalized by voter referendum
- 63% yes / 37% no — clear mandate
- Possession legal 30 days after passage (December 7, 2023)
- Retail licensing framework established

### December 2023: Gap Period Begins
- Possession legal; no legal point of purchase
- DCC tasked with building application system, processing dual-use conversions, conducting inspections, integrating METRC into adult-use framework
- Eight-month gap before retail launch creates black market pressure

### Early 2024: Legislative Response
- Republican supermajority introduces Senate Bill 261
- Proposed modifications: eliminate home cultivation, raise tax rate, lower THC potency limits, restructure regulatory authority, constrain new license issuance
- Most aggressive modifications scaled back but still introduced meaningful departures from voter-approved text

### August 2024: Retail Sales Launch
- First adult-use retail licenses issued
- Existing medical operators first in conversion queue
- Early inspection findings: camera gaps, signage issues, METRC reconfiguration
- DCC education-first enforcement posture for good-faith operators

### Late 2024–2025: Ongoing Legislative Pressure
- DCC implementing rules while rules remain subject to legislative amendment
- Home cultivation registration requirements operationalized (partially)
- METRC workflow complexity increases as dual-use operators scale
- Social equity provisions modified; implementation timelines extended

## Legislative Intent

### Issue 2 Goals (per Campaign)
- Legalize adult-use cannabis for adults 21+
- Create a regulated market with small business access
- Generate tax revenue (10% excise) for社会经济 programs
- Include social equity provisions to address disparate enforcement
- Allow home cultivation (6 plants/person, 12/household)

### Legislative Response
- Legislature consistently opposed Issue 2
- SB 261 amendments sought to eliminate home cultivation entirely
- Final modifications: registration requirements, security mandates, proximity restrictions
- Equity provisions diluted: eligibility criteria changed, implementation timelines extended

### The DCC's Position
- Standing up a dual-use regulatory apparatus from scratch
- Limited by ongoing legislative interference
- Education-first posture to allow market to stabilize
- Zero-tolerance carve-outs for serious violations from day one

## Key Policy Tensions

### Tension 1: Voter Mandate vs. Legislative Override

The fundamental conflict: Ohio voters approved Issue 2 by 63%; the legislature has spent every session since attempting to weaken or modify it. Operators must treat the Issue 2 statute, the SB 261 amendments, and the DCC's implementing rules as **three separate regulatory documents** — and any of them can change.

**Implication:** Compliance strategy cannot be separated from political intelligence. The regulatory landscape shifts monthly. Operators who treat ORC 3780 as settled law are working from an incomplete picture.

### Tension 2: Incumbent Advantage vs. Equity Access

Medical operators — who entered under HB 523, a program with no equity provisions — hold all the structural first-mover advantages: facility approvals, established supply chains, regulatory familiarity, capital access. The equity provisions in Issue 2 apply only to new licenses and provide fee reductions and priority processing, not capital or infrastructure.

**Implication:** A two-tier market is now structurally baked in. The equity program reduces friction at the margins but cannot retroactively restructure ownership of medical licensees who converted to dual-use.

### Tension 3: Tax Revenue vs. Market Viability

The 10% excise tax combined with standard sales tax pushes the consumer-facing rate above 17% in most jurisdictions. This creates pricing pressure relative to the unregulated market and raises the question of whether Ohio's licensed operators can compete with the black/grey market on price while carrying the compliance burden.

**Implication:** Operators who model margin around the 10% excise figure are materially miscalculating. The combined tax rate must be embedded in pricing strategy from day one.

### Tension 4: Local Control vs. Statewide Uniformity

Ohio municipalities can opt out of retail — creating a patchwork that fragments the commercial market, pushes operators toward urban cores and permissive rural areas, and creates unpredictability in the retail landscape. Local opt-out decisions can be made by ballot initiative or legislative action with no advance notice.

**Implication:** Site selection must include real-time municipal opt-out assessment. Delivery capability is a strategic hedge against opt-out jurisdictions.

### Tension 5: Medical-Use Integrity vs. Adult-Use Scale

Ohio's medical program under HB 523 was tightly regulated and small in scale. The adult-use market is substantially larger and more complex. Dual-use operators must maintain strict separation between medical and adult-use inventory in METRC — a compliance burden that adds operational complexity and cost.

**Implication:** METRC workflow reconfiguration is the central technical and compliance task of the dual-use transition. Operators who treat it as an IT upgrade rather than a compliance priority will fail inspection.
